Escalation Alert
February 2026

Supreme Court Strikes Down IEEPA Tariffs: Section 122 Replacement at 15% Effective February 24

Condition: Elevated
Coverage: All clients — U.S. imports (broad-based)
Impact: COST: HIGH • TIMING: LOW • EXECUTION: MODERATE
The administration replaced IEEPA tariffs with a 15% global tariff under Section 122, effective February 24 for 150 days. Section 301 and Section 232 tariffs remain in effect.
▾
Assessed impact

Cost: Most goods shift to 15% Section 122 plus applicable Section 301 duties; landed cost will likely require a SKU-level refresh.

Timing: No immediate clearance disruption expected, but guidance could tighten near Feb 24.

Execution: Exemptions may apply (USMCA goods, pharmaceuticals, critical minerals, certain electronics); unverified SKUs may be treated as dutiable pending confirmation.

Decision window

EOD February 23: Actions to consider before the February 24 effective date.

Before Feb 24, consider validating exemption status for in-scope SKUs, refreshing landed cost math using 15% Section 122 plus applicable Section 301 duties, and consulting trade counsel on potential IEEPA refund eligibility while compiling 2025 duty records by HTS code.

Watchpoints

CBP implementation: Look for CSMS messages, new documentation requirements, or port-level clearance friction tied to Section 122 starting Feb 24.

Refund path: Watch for Treasury/CBP instructions or CIT filings that define eligibility, procedure, and timing.

Sources
  1. Supreme Court: Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (opinion PDF) — Feb 20, 2026.
  2. White House: Proclamation — Section 122 temporary import surcharge — effective Feb 24, 2026 (150 days).
  3. White House: Executive Order — ending certain tariff actions — IEEPA duties terminated.
  4. CBP: CSMS #67834313 — ending collection of IEEPA duties — ACE/HTS guidance.
  5. Counsel Note: WilmerHale client alert — practical implications.
Escalation Alert
January 2026

Midwest winter storm disrupts rail and intermodal: transit time volatility across central corridors

Condition: Disrupted
Coverage: U.S. inland rail/intermodal — central corridors and key intermodal hubs
Impact: COST: MODERATE • TIMING: HIGH • EXECUTION: HIGH
Severe winter weather prompted railroads to warn customers to expect delays, longer transit times, and irregular routings as networks protect operations and recover backlogs. The practical impact is missed delivery windows and bunching at destination terminals as volume releases unevenly.
▾
Assessed impact

Cost: Secondary costs show up as detention/storage, premium drayage, and recovery fees when freight bunches after weather holds.

Timing: Treat ETAs as ranges. Delays can persist beyond the storm window while terminals dig out backlogs.

Execution: Expect irregular routings, atypical interchanges, gate/appointment congestion, and uneven container availability during recovery.

Decision window

Next 3–7 days: The recovery phase is typically where planning breaks (bunching and missed appointments).

For priority containers, pre-book drayage with wider pickup windows, confirm terminal appointment posture, and align internal expectations that delivery promises may need buffer during network normalization.

Watchpoints

Rail customer advisories: “full recovery” language and specific intermodal hub constraints.

Terminal signals: gate closures, appointment backlogs, and extended free time announcements.

Downstream effects: bunching at DCs and missed rail connections that widen variability for 1–2 weeks.

Sources
  1. Reuters: BNSF warns winter storm will snarl rail traffic across central U.S. corridors — Jan 22, 2026.
  2. BNSF: Customer notification — major winter storm expected to impact network — Jan 22, 2026.
Escalation Alert
September 2025

Super Typhoon Ragasa triggers port and terminal suspensions across South China and Hong Kong

Condition: Critical
Coverage: Asia exports via South China/Hong Kong gateways — vessel schedules, feeder connections, and regional rollover risk
Impact: COST: MODERATE • TIMING: HIGH • EXECUTION: HIGH
Multiple major South China and Hong Kong terminals suspended operations ahead of Ragasa, driving immediate vessel/feeder disruption and a rolling backlog. Knock-on effects typically show up as rolled containers, missed transshipment connections, and multi-day schedule recovery.
▾
Assessed impact

Cost: Limited direct cost unless mode shifts are required; secondary costs show up as premium trucking and schedule recovery fees.

Timing: Expect multi-day variability beyond the closure window while ports clear backlogs and vessels re-sequence calls.

Execution: Highest risk is rolled cargo, missed feeder connections, and unstable ETAs across downstream Asian gateways.

Decision window

Next 72 hours: Confirm which sailings are impacted and whether carriers are omitting/re-sequencing port calls.

For time-sensitive freight, validate transshipment plans and cutoffs, and treat ETAs as bands until port operations fully normalize.

Watchpoints

Terminal restart: confirmation of resumed gate/vessel operations and backlog clearance pace.

Carrier advisories: omitted calls, rolled bookings, and revised pro-forma schedules.

Regional spillover: congestion signals at alternative hubs as volume reroutes.

Sources
  1. S&P Global: Super Typhoon Ragasa disrupts China’s key ports, sparking regional ripple effects — Sep 22, 2025.
  2. SEKO Logistics: Port suspensions and expected delay ranges (South China / Hong Kong) — Sep 25, 2025.
  3. ISS Shipping: Advisory — Hong Kong port operations disruption and resumption updates — Sep 23–25, 2025.
  4. Reuters: Hong Kong reopens after Cyclone Ragasa — Sep 25, 2025.
Escalation Alert
August 2024

Port of Seattle ransomware incident: systems outage and manual workarounds at maritime and airport facilities

Condition: Disrupted
Coverage: U.S. gateways — ports/airports with shared IT dependencies; shippers with tight appointment windows
Impact: COST: MODERATE • TIMING: MODERATE • EXECUTION: HIGH
The Port of Seattle reported a ransomware incident and took systems offline as response actions were implemented. Operationally, cyber events at port operators typically create release/appointment friction, documentation delays, and manual workarounds until systems stabilize.
▾
Assessed impact

Cost: Secondary costs include storage, demurrage/detention, and premium drayage when appointment and release processes slow.

Timing: Variability increases; treat terminal/warehouse planning as ranges until systems normalize.

Execution: Highest risk is operational friction (release, appointments, documentation), not physical capacity.

Decision window

Next 48–72 hours: Confirm status and workaround posture with terminals, carriers, and drayage providers.

For time-sensitive moves, pre-align on manual procedures and build buffer for pickup/return windows until normal automation resumes.

Watchpoints

Restoration updates: whether port operator reports stable recovery and normal processing.

Terminal advisories: appointment/release changes, EDI/manual filing requirements, and extended free time language.

Sources
  1. Port of Seattle: Port Cyberattack Archive (incident timeline and updates) — Aug 2024.
  2. Port of Seattle: Notice to individuals affected (data impact confirmation) — Apr 3, 2025.
Standing Advisory (Monthly Monitoring Module)
February 2026

Mexico overland disruption and cargo security — tequila imports (standing advisory)

Condition: Elevated
Coverage: U.S. tequila importer — Mexico overland legs to U.S. border crossings (lane-dependent)
Impact: COST: MODERATE • TIMING: MODERATE • EXECUTION: MODERATE
This standing advisory is maintained as part of ongoing monitoring and referenced in monthly updates. Overland corridors can experience sudden disruption and elevated cargo theft risk, producing transit time volatility and carrier constraints for high-value spirits.
▾
Operational relevance

Timing: pickup/linehaul delays and missed border windows can cascade into late delivery and inventory gaps.

Cost: premium trucking/security requirements, detention risk from missed appointments, and tighter insurance constraints for spirits.

Execution: carrier refusals, reroutes, and changing security requirements can alter planning assumptions quickly.

Monitoring signals

Embassy security alerts: changes in on-the-ground security posture and corridor disruption indicators.

Border signals: unusual wait-time spikes at key commercial crossings.

Theft trends: corridor/state trend reporting used to update posture in monthly outlooks.

Escalation triggers (becomes an EA if…)

Sustained corridor disruption: verified blockades/unrest affecting primary lanes or crossings.

Posture shift: credible reporting of elevated theft risk affecting spirits/high-value cargo on relevant corridors.

Carrier constraint: capacity tightening or new security/insurance requirements that materially change cost or timing.

  2. CBP: Border Wait Times (port-by-port) .
  3. Overhaul: Mexico cargo theft reporting (trend input) .
